Slip-and-fall and dog-bite claims both live under California personal injury law, but they are built from different legal parts — one turns on property conditions and notice, the other on strict statutory liability and a homeowner's policy. We evaluated more than 30 California firms on how well they actually handle each case type, and how clearly they can explain the difference before you sign anything. This guide is for anyone hurt on someone else's property or bitten by someone else's dog who wants the right advocate for the facts they actually have.

What to Consider

Case-type fit: premises liability versus animal liability. A slip-and-fall is a premises case. You have to prove the owner had a duty, that a dangerous condition existed, and — critically — that the owner knew or should have known about it. A dog bite in California is usually simpler on liability: Civil Code § 3342 imposes strict liability on the owner for bites in a public place or while you are lawfully on private property. That means you want a lawyer who can pivot between a fault-based theory and a strict-liability theory without stretching either one.

Evidence and investigation demands. Premises cases are evidence races against the clock. Surveillance video gets overwritten in days, spill logs vanish, and maintenance records get "archived." Dog-bite cases hinge on different proof: animal control reports, prior-bite history, leash-law compliance, and the identity of every policy that might respond. Ask any firm what they do in the first 72 hours — the answer tells you more than the website does.

Who actually pays the claim. In a slip-and-fall, you are usually dealing with a commercial general liability carrier, a property management company, or a public entity. In a dog-bite case, it is almost always a homeowner's or renter's policy — which means coverage limits may be modest and the insurer may argue the dog had no vicious propensity or that you provoked it. A firm that understands policy limits and excess coverage will tell you early whether the case is worth pursuing.

Deadlines and immunities. California gives you two years from the injury to file a personal injury suit under Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1. But if you slipped on public property — a sidewalk, a transit platform, a city parking structure — you must present a government claim within six months under Government Code § 911.2. Miss it and the case is gone. Dog-bite claims rarely involve that trap, but they do involve comparative fault and, sometimes, a landlord who claims no knowledge of the animal.

Fee structure and communication fit. Nearly every firm in this space works on contingency, so the real differentiators are responsiveness, language access, whether you get the attorney or an intake specialist, and whether the firm fronts costs for experts. Ask who answers the phone on a Saturday, and ask whether the fee agreement covers expert costs or deducts them from your share.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a dog bite in California require proving the owner was negligent?

Usually not. Civil Code § 3342 makes the owner strictly liable for bites that occur in a public place or while you are lawfully on private property, regardless of whether the dog had bitten anyone before. That is the key structural difference from a slip-and-fall, where you must prove the property owner had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition. Practical defense fights in bite cases tend to center on provocation, trespass, and whether the person was lawfully present.

How long do I have to file a slip-and-fall claim in California?

Two years from the date of injury under Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 for a private property claim. If the fall happened on public property — a city sidewalk, a transit station, a government building — you must first present a claim to the public entity within six months under Government Code § 911.2, and the timeline can be unforgiving. This is the single biggest reason premises cases need a lawyer early rather than after treatment ends.

Can one lawyer handle both a slip-and-fall and a dog-bite claim?

Yes, and many California personal injury firms do. What separates the good ones is whether they recognize the theories are different: notice and dangerous condition on the premises side, strict statutory liability and policy limits on the animal side. Ask a firm directly how their approach differs between the two. If the answer sounds generic, keep looking.

Will I owe anything if my case does not settle or win?

Under a standard contingency arrangement, you owe no attorney fee unless compensation is recovered — that is the norm across the firms in this guide. Costs are the detail to check. Ask whether expert fees and filing costs are advanced by the firm and reimbursed from the recovery, or billed to you regardless of outcome, and get the answer in writing.
